01 Project Brief

What is Facts?

Facts is a mobile app that helps everyday readers slow down and check what they’re about to share. Instead of policing speech, it builds one simple habit: pause, verify, decide. Misinformation then loses its single biggest advantage, speed.

The goal: make fact-checking feel effortless and non-judgemental, so it fits naturally into the few seconds before someone taps “share”.

02 The Challenge

Why misinformation wins

Misinformation spreads faster than the truth because sharing is frictionless and emotional. By the time a correction arrives, the false claim has already travelled, and been believed.

  • Speed beats accuracy. A claim is reshared in seconds, long before anyone checks it.
  • Emotion drives sharing. Outrage and fear travel further than nuance.
  • No moment to pause. Nothing prompts people to question a post before they tap share.
  • Fact-checks feel like lectures. Corrections arrive late and come across as preachy.
  • Trust is fragmented. People genuinely don’t know which sources to believe.

Understanding how it spreads

I combined secondary research on how misinformation travels with primary work, interviews and a survey, to understand when, why, and how people share before they verify.

The internet leads to addiction, addiction leads to belief and manipulation, all as one connected system. Drop misinformation into that system and it can look harmless, until it goes viral.

01

What gives rise to misinformation?

  • Lack of regulation on news handles. There are no entry barriers for information on social media.
  • Communal polarisation. Society is largely split along ideological lines, which makes misinformation easy to spread.
  • Reach. As internet use grows, so does people’s reach, and the mobile phone became the catalyst.
  • Quick gain. Misinformation is advertised for money.
02

How people respond to misinformation

  • Rebuttal. Collecting facts to confirm the information isn’t fake.
  • Removal. Taking down misinformation by reporting it.
  • Educating others. Presenting facts so people can see the truth for themselves.
03

Challenges in fighting misinformation

  • The misinformation factory. You might rebut one fake instance, but the machine keeps churning out similar stories to push its narrative.
  • People rarely verify. Studies show most people never check misinformation; they reach for the evidence they already believe is right.
  • Lazy thinking. We are, by default, lazy thinkers.

04 Insights

From noise to a direction

01 · Key insights

Surveys and interviews kept pointing to the same gap: people know misinformation is everywhere, but the moment they meet it, checking feels too slow and too uncertain to bother. Four insights shaped the direction.

Insight 01

Nearly everyone is exposed, almost no one acts. 96% come across misinformation and 82% call it a big problem, yet only 67% ever try to verify. Awareness is not the issue, effort is.

Insight 02

Verification loses the race against speed. A post spreads in seconds, but checking it means digging through too many scattered, low-trust sources, so people fall back on the belief they already hold.

Insight 03

People want to help, not just stay safe. Across personas, users didn't only want to protect themselves, they wanted to collect facts and educate others. The design has to let them act on the truth, not just spot a lie.

Insight 04

Trust is the real currency. With no single credible source, a verdict only lands if it feels authentic and neutral, and if getting it is low-effort and close to one-touch.

03 · Design statement

Everything converged into one statement, the Why, Who, and How, that anchored the rest of the project.

Design statement

For people who share and shape opinion on social media, Facts is a digital platform that makes verifying any claim, whether image, text, or video, quick and effortless before it is shared. Anyone can request a check or tap into information already verified by AI after comparing many articles, keeping the facts open for all.

06 Reflection

What I learned

The research kept saying the same thing: people are not malicious, they are just moving fast. 96% run into misinformation but only 67% ever check, because verifying feels slow and effortful. That reframed the whole brief. The job was not to block or shame anyone at the point of sharing, it was to slip a lightweight pause into a habit they already have. A one-tap popover that scans a post in place respects how little time and patience people have, and that is exactly what makes it likely to get used.

People told me plainly that they do not know which source to believe and that facts often do not feel authentic. So a verdict on its own was never going to be enough. The design had to carry its own credibility: show what the AI compared, surface the evidence behind a result, and let people see how a conclusion was reached rather than just asserting it. Designing for trust changed small decisions everywhere, from how a result is worded to how its sources are shown.

Existing fact-checks read like lectures, and the people I spoke to switched off the moment they felt judged. Keeping the voice calm, neutral and non-judgemental turned out to matter as much as speed or accuracy. When the product feels like a helpful companion rather than a scold, people are far more willing to slow down, check, and even pass the correction on. Tone is not decoration, it is what keeps the door open.
